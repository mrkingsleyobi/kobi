A bug bounty program doesn't pay you for scanning. It pays you for a valid finding.

You can spend a week on a target, write a beautiful methodology document, and earn nothing. Or you can spend an afternoon, find one real vulnerability, and get paid. The market only cares whether something that was broken is now known to be broken.

I learned to work inside that rule before I ever called myself an AI consultant. I think AI consulting should work the same way.

## What most AI work sells today

Most AI engagements end with an artifact about the work instead of the work itself: a strategy deck, a roadmap, a list of "use cases," a workshop where everyone leaves excited.

None of these are useless, but none of them change anything on their own. The invoice is paid when the slides are delivered, and nobody checks six months later whether the process actually got better.

That's the gap I care about. The client paid for a presentation when what they needed was an outcome.

## The unit of value is an improved outcome

My rule is simple: measure AI by outcomes, not presentations.

An outcome is a number that was one thing before the work and a better thing after it.

It might be the time to answer a customer ticket, the cost per document processed, the error rate on a review step, or the hours a team spends on work nobody should be doing by hand.

If you can't name the number before you start, you don't have an engagement yet, just a conversation about one.

## Why this is harder, and why that's the point

Selling outcomes is uncomfortable. You take on risk the deck-seller never takes: the data might be worse than promised, the team might not adopt the tool, the model might not be good enough for the job.

I think that discomfort is the filter. It forces you to scope honestly, test early, and walk away from work you can't actually move.

It also forces verification. You don't get to say "it should work." You show the before and the after, and the difference between them is what you delivered.

Security people already think this way. A pentest report full of theoretical risks is worth less than one confirmed, reproducible finding. AI work deserves the same standard.

## What changes when you work like this

Working this way changes how I run an engagement more than which model I pick.

I scope smaller: one process, one number, one owner. A narrow win that's measured beats a broad transformation that isn't.

I build what lasts. If an engagement only produces my hours, it ends when I stop. If it produces a tool, an agent, or a method the client keeps running, the outcome keeps paying after I leave.

That's also how I plan to stop selling time and start owning the systems that encode the expertise.

I publish what I measure. A claim like "we cut processing time" is marketing. "We cut it from this to this, and here's how" is evidence other people can check.

## The question to ask

If you're hiring someone to "do AI" for your business, ask one thing before you sign: what number will be different when you're done, and how will we both know?

If the answer is a deck, you're buying a presentation. That might be what you want. Just don't confuse it with an outcome.
