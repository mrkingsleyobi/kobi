<callout>Everyone wants to be in the center of the action. But sometimes the most important growth happens when you leave.</callout>

Three years ago, I did what everyone said you shouldn't do: I left Silicon Valley.

I had a great job at a hot startup, impressive compensation, and access to the most interesting technology conversations on the planet. I was exactly where ambitious technologists are supposed to want to be.

And I was miserable.

---

## The Bubble Problem

Living in Silicon Valley, you lose perspective.

<aside>When everyone around you is building the next big thing, you stop asking whether it should be built at all.</aside>

**The bubble distorts:**

- **What matters**: IPOs and funding rounds become proxies for impact
- **Who matters**: Proximity to power replaces actual influence
- **Success looks like**: Optimizing for status instead of meaning

I found myself caring deeply about problems that didn't matter, optimized for metrics that didn't align with my values, and surrounded by people who thought this was normal.

---

## The Breaking Point

<tutorial>Pay attention to what you're optimizing for. If you can't remember why it matters, you're optimizing for the wrong thing.</tutorial>

The moment I knew I had to leave:

I was at a networking event where someone asked me what I was working on. I gave my practiced pitch about changing the world through technology. They nodded and said, "That's great, but what do you actually care about?"

I didn't have an answer.

I'd spent years optimizing my career for external validation — prestigious companies, impressive titles, growing compensation — but I'd lost touch with what actually mattered to me.

<callout>The most dangerous trap isn't failing to achieve your goals — it's achieving goals that don't matter.</callout>

---

## The Decision

Leaving meant:

- Giving up the prestige of being "in Silicon Valley"
- Taking a significant pay cut
- Moving away from friends and professional network
- Admitting that the path everyone said was right wasn't right for me

It was terrifying.

---

## What I Found Outside

<aside>Distance from the center of action gives you clarity about what action is worth taking.</aside>

**What I gained:**

1. **Perspective**: Away from the constant hype cycle, I could think clearly about what technology actually matters
2. **Balance**: Work became one part of life, not the entire thing
3. **Authentic connections**: People outside tech are more interesting and grounded
4. **Time to think**: Not constantly optimizing for next opportunity created space for actual reflection

**What I learned:**

- You don't need to be in Silicon Valley to do important work
- The most interesting technology conversations are happening everywhere now
- Status is a trap — impact is what matters
- Your location doesn't determine your worth

---

## The Trade-offs

Let me be clear: leaving wasn't all upside.

**I miss:**

- Serendipitous conversations with brilliant people
- Easy access to cutting-edge technology
- The energy of being surrounded by ambitious builders
- Certain types of opportunities that cluster in the Bay Area

**But I gained:**

- Mental health and life balance
- Deeper relationships outside of work
- Perspective on what actually matters
- The ability to work on things I genuinely care about

---

## Advice for Others

<tutorial>The right environment for someone else might be wrong for you. Stop optimizing for what looks good and start optimizing for what feels right.</tutorial>

**Questions to ask yourself:**

1. Are you optimizing for status or impact?
2. Do you like who you're becoming in your current environment?
3. What would you do if no one was watching?
4. What's the worst thing that happens if you leave?

**Signs it might be time to go:**

- You feel like you're performing instead of being
- Your values don't align with your environment
- You've stopped asking "why"
- You're staying for the wrong reasons

---

## The Bottom Line

<callout>Location matters less than ever. What matters is whether you're doing work that matters to you, with people you respect, in a way that aligns with your values.</callout>

Leaving Silicon Valley was the best career decision I ever made.

Not because where I am is objectively better — but because it's better *for me*.

The goal isn't to be in the right place. The goal is to be in the place that's right for you.
